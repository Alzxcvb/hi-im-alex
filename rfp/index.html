How it works

  1. You send the RFP and your files. Your last three submittals, resumes and project sheets. It needs to be due at least five business days out, and if two firms ask for the same solicitation, the first written yes gets it.
  2. I aim to send a draft two business days after I have those. Every section I can write, in the order the solicitation asks for, a compliance checklist of what is mine and what is yours to add, and a one page note on how well the job fits your firm.
  3. Your team edits and submits. It stays your submission. I never contact the agency about it.

What you get out of it

  • Go after more work. With the first draft off your plate, you can chase the ones you pass on today for lack of time.
  • Keep your engineers billable. The federal paperwork estimate for filling out an SF 330 is 29 hours per response. Your people work from a draft instead of a blank page. I do not promise a set number of hours saved, and the debrief is how we measure it.

I do not promise a win or a shortlist. No one can.

Why the fee never depends on winning

Rules on paying an outside party a fee that depends on winning a public contract are strict in many places, and the risk can land on your firm. So my fee never depends on an award, a shortlist or an interview. It depends on one thing you control: whether what you submit is mostly my text.

If an agency rejects a submission in writing for missing something the checklist marked as mine, I redo your next draft for free.
